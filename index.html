At a Glance

A Small Town with Big Questions

Little Compton is one of the smallest and most rural communities in Rhode Island — a quiet corner of Newport County where farmland meets the sea. With roughly 3,600 year-round residents and a deep reliance on property taxes, every dollar in the town budget carries real weight. Here's how it all adds up.

Revenue

Where the Money Comes From

Little Compton's revenue picture is unusually simple — and unusually concentrated. In FY2024, property taxes made up 95% of all general fund revenue. State aid, fees, and other sources account for the remaining sliver. This heavy reliance on property taxes means that when the tax base shifts, the whole budget shifts with it.

FY2026: A 12% Levy Increase on the Table

State law normally caps yearly property tax levy increases at 4%. But facing a $1M structural deficit, Little Compton's Town Council unanimously voted to seek state approval for a one-time 12% levy increase for FY2026 — triple the usual cap. The request (RI House Bill H6362) requires approval at the Financial Town Meeting.

Spending

Where the Money Goes

Over half of every dollar Little Compton spends goes to education. Public safety — police and fire/rescue — takes the next largest share, followed by general government operations. Despite the town's small size, these are the fixed costs that drive the budget year after year.

Education

The Biggest Line Item, by Far

Education commands 55% of the total budget. Little Compton operates one K–8 school (Wilbur & McMahon School) with about 201 students. The town has no high school — roughly 81 students are tuitioned out to neighboring districts each year, a significant cost driver.

Why Is Per-Pupil Spending So High?

Small schools face a classic diseconomy of scale. With only about 200 students, Little Compton still needs a principal, custodians, bus routes, special-education services, and a full complement of teachers — costs that larger districts spread across thousands of pupils. The budget committee has pointed to "increased hiring of staff and teachers in the face of a dropping rate of enrollment" as a pressure point.

Meanwhile, the school board has pushed back, noting that figures cited in budget debates have sometimes been inaccurate and that the true per-pupil number depends on how you count the ~81 tuitioned-out high school students.

Fiscal Health

The Shrinking Safety Net

The story of Little Compton's recent finances is, in large part, the story of a dwindling general fund balance. For years, the town used reserves to avoid raising taxes. That strategy has now caught up with it.

General Fund Balance

General fund balance dropped 37% in a single year (FY2023 to FY2024), from $3.17M to $1.98M.

School Fund Balance

School unrestricted fund fell 32% year-over-year.

How a "Perfect Storm" Built Up

FY2023
No property tax levy increase. Town begins "increasingly relying" on general fund reserves to cover recurring operational expenses.
FY2024
Again, no levy increase. The town draws over $1 million from the general fund to balance the budget. Fund balance drops to $1.98M.
FY2025
A 3.92% levy increase is enacted, but it's not enough to close the gap. The structural deficit persists.
FY2026
Officials propose an $18.3M spending plan, but discover the real number is closer to $19M. A 12% levy override is sought from the state legislature (H6362). A property revaluation raises the town's tax base from $2.7B to $3.29B, partially softening the rate increase for homeowners.
FY2027
Draft budget now posted on the town website. The path forward remains uncertain.
⚠

The Net Position Is Negative

As of June 30, 2024, the town's total assets plus deferred inflows fell short of its liabilities plus deferred outflows by $1.68 million, resulting in a negative net position of −$1,677,993. This figure includes long-term obligations like pension and OPEB liabilities.

Context

Little Compton is wealthy, older, and highly educated compared to both Rhode Island and the U.S. as a whole. Many homes are seasonal. These demographics shape both the tax base and the demands placed on town services.

Looking Ahead

What to Watch

The Levy Override Vote

If approved by the state legislature and then by voters at the Financial Town Meeting (scheduled for June 17, 2025), the one-time 12% levy increase would bring in significantly more revenue. If it fails, deeper cuts will be needed.

Post-Revaluation Adjustments

The 2024 revaluation raised the town's total assessed value by ~22% (to $3.29B). While the tax rate drops from $5.08 to ~$4.70, individual bills could rise if a property's assessment grew faster than average.

School Enrollment & Costs

With only ~200 K–8 students and ~81 tuitioned-out high schoolers, education costs per pupil will remain high. Any further enrollment declines could push per-pupil spending even higher.
