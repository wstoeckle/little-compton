At a Glance

A Small Town with Big Questions

Little Compton is one of the smallest and most rural communities in Rhode Island — a quiet corner of Newport County where farmland meets the sea. With roughly 3,600 year-round residents and a deep reliance on property taxes, every dollar in the town budget carries real weight. Here's how it all adds up.

Little Compton's revenue picture is concentrated but not quite as extreme as earlier estimates suggested. Intergovernmental aid was 5.6%, licenses/fees/beach receipts were 5.3%, and investment/miscellaneous income was 0.5%.

FY2026: Why the Levy Debate Escalated

State law normally caps yearly property-tax levy increases at 4%. During the FY2026 cycle, officials sought state approval for a one-time 12% override after discovering a deeper structural gap than expected and cutting spending to close it.

Education

The Biggest Line Item, by Far

Education remains the biggest line item, but the deficit story is not just "school spending exploded." The town appropriation to schools rose from $7,638,456 (FY2023) to $7,815,947 (FY2024), while the general fund absorbed broader cost pressure and reserve drawdowns.

Why Is Per-Pupil Spending So High?

Small schools face a classic diseconomy of scale. With just over 200 K–8 students, fixed staffing and facility costs are spread across a small enrollment base. Enrollment appears to have ticked up (from roughly 201 to 208), so recent pressure is better explained by cost inflation than by falling enrollment.

Audit schedules show instructional and administrative compensation increasing year-over-year, while purchased services remained large. That pattern supports a broader personnel-and-operations cost story, not a sudden education-only spike.

Fiscal Health

The Shrinking Safety Net

The story of Little Compton's recent finances is, in large part, the story of a dwindling general fund balance. For years, the town used reserves to avoid raising taxes. That strategy has now caught up with it.

General Fund Balance

General fund balance dropped $1,184,291 in one year, from $3,165,258 (FY2023) to $1,980,967 (FY2024).

School Fund Balance

School unrestricted fund fell from $704,431 (FY2023) to $478,458 (FY2024).

How a "Perfect Storm" Built Up

FY2023
No property-tax levy increase. Officials later described growing reliance on general fund reserves to cover recurring operations.
FY2024
Again, no levy increase. General fund revenue rises only $247,913 year-over-year while general fund expenditures rise $1,232,973. Fund balance falls to $1,980,967.
FY2025
A 3.92% levy increase is enacted, but structural pressure remains. Budget committee documents show large increases in Active Health Benefits (+$121,489) and Pension (+$125,407) versus FY2025 approved levels.
FY2026
Officials initially forwarded an $18.3M plan, then identified a computational error that left the total roughly $600,000 short in earlier drafts; revised figures moved closer to $19M. Public reporting says the error occurred before then-town administrator Antonio Teixeira transmitted the plan. A 12% levy override is sought while the town revaluation lifts the tax base from $2.70B to $3.29B.
FY2027
FY2027 draft budget and warrant format are posted publicly, enabling line-item review and comparison against prior audited years.

What Actually Drove the Deficit?

The clearest pattern in the audited data is a revenue-cost mismatch: two straight years with no levy increase, modest revenue growth, and much faster spending growth. From FY2023 to FY2024, general fund revenue increased only 1.7% while general fund expenditures increased 16.8%.

Personnel-related pressure is also visible in the town's own budget records: salaries and health insurance are explicitly identified as major drivers, with health and dental premiums projected up 10% and 5%, and pension/benefit lines rising sharply in FY2026 recommendations. The published reporting also identifies a roughly $600,000 computational budget error in the FY2026 cycle, while the FY2024 audit attributes control failures to finance-department turnover and does not assign fault to one named individual. Taken together, this points to a structural revenue and cost-control problem, not a sudden education-only spike.

⚠

The Net Position Is Negative

As of June 30, 2024, the town reported a negative net position of −$561,197, after a year-over-year decline of about $3.49M. This reflects long-term obligations, including pension and OPEB liabilities, in addition to annual operating results.

Context

Who Lives Here

Little Compton is wealthy, older, and highly educated compared to both Rhode Island and the U.S. as a whole. Many homes are seasonal. These demographics shape both the tax base and the demands placed on town services.

Looking Ahead

What to Watch

The Levy Override Vote

If approved by the state legislature and then by voters at the Financial Town Meeting (scheduled for June 17, 2025), the one-time 12% levy increase would bring in significantly more revenue. If it fails, deeper cuts will be needed.

Post-Revaluation Adjustments

The 2024 revaluation raised the town's total assessed value by ~22% (to $3.29B). While the tax rate dropped from $5.08 to $4.79, individual bills can still rise if a property's assessment grows faster than average.

School Enrollment & Costs

With around 208 K–8 students and 81 tuitioned-out high schoolers, per-pupil costs remain structurally high due to fixed staffing and facility costs. Recent pressure is more about cost inflation than enrollment collapse.
